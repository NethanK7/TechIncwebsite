Nobody chooses spreadsheets. They accumulate. One file for the stock count because the system did not have batches. One for the production plan because the planner needed something flexible. One for the management pack because neither of the first two reconciled.

The cost is real, it is just never on an invoice. Here is where we consistently find it.

## Reconciliation labour

The most visible cost. Someone — usually a good, expensive person in finance — spends the first week of every month making three sources agree. That is not analysis. That is data entry with extra steps.

In the implementations we have delivered, month-end closing consistently gets shorter — not because the work got faster, but because it stopped existing: when every transaction posts itself to the ledger as it happens, closing becomes a review rather than a reconstruction.

## Decisions on stale numbers

A spreadsheet is a photograph of a moment that has already passed. If your stock position is a file someone updates on Fridays, then every purchasing decision made on a Wednesday is made on four-day-old data.

This is where the money actually goes, and it is the hardest cost to see. Excess stock that nobody knew was there. A stockout on the one SKU that mattered. A customer promised a delivery the warehouse could not make.

Excess stock is the clearest version of this: money sitting on a shelf because nobody had a live, reliable position to reorder against. See our [Electro-Serv Lanka case study](/case-studies/electro-serv-lanka) for what improved inventory visibility looks like once distribution, manufacturing and warehousing read from the same system.

## The single point of failure

Every spreadsheet-run business has one person who understands the model. They built it, they maintain the formulas, and they are the only one who knows why row 40 has a hard-coded adjustment.

That is an operational risk with no mitigation. It is also unfair to them.

## Cost that only shows up when you grow

Spreadsheets scale linearly with effort. Doubling transaction volume doubles the entry, doubles the reconciliation, doubles the error surface. A system scales sub-linearly — mostly it just runs.

This is why the spreadsheet cost is so often invisible until a growth year, at which point it arrives all at once, usually as a hiring request.

## What to do about it

Start by counting. How many hours a month go into making numbers agree? What was the last decision made on a figure someone had to phone up to confirm? How long does month-end take?
