Most ERP implementations that fail don't fail on technology. They fail because the whole project was scheduled to arrive on one date, and the date arrived before anyone actually understood the requirement.

NXTGEN is our answer to that, refined over more than 30 implementations. It has five phases, and the second one is where most of the risk leaves the project.

## Design: map what happens, not what should

We map processes as they actually run, not as the org chart or the SOP claims they do. That gap is where most surprises come from, and it's much cheaper to find them now than in month nine.

The output is a target state designed against what Frappe can already do natively. Where a gap is real, we plan to build for it. Where it's just a habit, we say so.

## Segregate: the phase that removes the risk

Scope gets split into modules that can be delivered independently, each with its own acceptance criteria.

That sounds administrative. It's actually the single most important structural decision in the whole programme, because it changes what can go wrong:

- No module waits on every other module. Accounting can be accepted while manufacturing is still being configured.
- A problem in one module stays contained there. It delays that module's gate, not the go-live.
- Progress becomes measurable in delivered function instead of percentage-complete estimates, which are basically guesses dressed up in a spreadsheet.

A big-bang go-live has one failure mode, and it's total. Six gated modules have six small ones.

## Cyclic Mapping: short loops, owners in the room

Configure, review, refine, with the people who own the process actually present, looking at the working system on their own data.

The point isn't speed. It's that a misunderstood requirement shows up in week four instead of at go-live. The cost of changing a requirement rises steeply the longer it sits unnoticed, and this loop is what catches most of them while they're still cheap to fix.

It also builds ownership. A process owner who watched their workflow get shaped is a very different stakeholder than one who was just handed a finished system.

## Training: adoption is the deliverable

Role-based training, on your own data, on your own workflows. Documentation of how your team actually works, not the vendor's generic manual.

We build internal champions on purpose, because the goal is a team that can answer its own questions six months from now. An ERP nobody uses isn't an implementation. It's an expense. And the failure mode is quiet: people drift back to the spreadsheet and nobody reports it, because nobody wants to be the one who admits it.

## Go-Live Authorization: a gate, not a date

Data migration verified against source balances. Parallel runs reconciled. Sign-off from every process owner.

We don't go live on a date. We go live when the gate is genuinely clear. Saying that out loud early changes the conversation, because it makes readiness the shared goal instead of the calendar.

## What it adds up to

Deployment time down by up to 40% against conventional approaches, on a 12-week standard programme, with 100% client satisfaction across the portfolio so far.

The speed is a side effect, not the aim. Segregated scope with real gates is just a more efficient way to work than one monolithic push toward an immovable date.
